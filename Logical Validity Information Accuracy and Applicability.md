# Logical Validity, Information Accuracy, and Applicability

## Principle

A line of reasoning may be logically valid while being inapplicable to the actual situation because one or more premises, classifications, or background assumptions are inaccurate.

Logical validity concerns the relationship between premises and conclusions. Factual accuracy concerns whether the premises correspond to reality. Applicability concerns whether the premises, assumptions, and scope conditions appropriately describe the situation being analyzed.

These properties must be assessed separately.

## Evaluation procedure

For each significant inference:

1. Record the premises and conclusion.
2. Identify the inference rule or reasoning method.
3. Assess the accuracy, provenance, completeness, and currency of each premise.
4. Identify assumptions and conditions required for the inference to apply.
5. Determine whether the actual situation satisfies those conditions.
6. Identify alternative explanations and potentially disconfirming evidence.
7. Reassess the conclusion when relevant information changes.
8. Preserve the original reasoning and document subsequent revisions.

## Required distinctions

* A valid inference is not necessarily based on true premises.
* True premises do not guarantee a valid inference.
* An argument is sound when it is deductively valid and its premises are true.
* A reasonable provisional inference may remain uncertain because its evidence is incomplete.
* A conclusion that is inapplicable under current circumstances does not necessarily establish that the underlying reasoning method is defective.

## Reporting standard

For each finding, distinguish:

* verified facts;
* unverified or disputed information;
* assumptions;
* inference rules;
* applicability conditions;
* alternative explanations;
* conclusions and confidence limitations.

Do not treat logical coherence as proof of factual accuracy. Do not treat an inaccurate premise as proof that every subsequent inference rule is defective.
